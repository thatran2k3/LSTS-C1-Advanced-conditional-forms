Advanced Conditional Forms

Supposing / Imagine

We often use supposing or imagine instead of if in spoken situations. This suggests an act of imagination.

Examples

  • Supposing you could go anywhere? Where would you go?
  • Imagine you won the lottery. What would you buy first?

Check your understanding

1. Ask someone to imagine losing their keys right now.
Supposing you lost your keys right now? What would you do?
2. Ask someone to imagine having to move to another country tomorrow.
Imagine you had to move to another country tomorrow. How would you feel?

Unless

Unless tends to be used more in real conditionals and means if not. We don't use unless in questions.

Examples

  • Unless we change the way we think about work, we'll never be able to face the challenges of the 21st century.

Check your understanding

1. If you don't study, you will fail the test.
Unless you study, you will fail the test.
2. I won't go to the party if she doesn't invite me.
I won't go to the party unless she invites me.

As long as / So long as / Provided / Providing

These phrases mean "if" or "on condition that".

Examples

  • You can borrow my car { as long as / so long as } you promise not to drive too fast. (= you can borrow my car, but you must promise not to drive too fast – this is a condition)
  • Provided / As long as the stadium is finished, the games will go ahead as planned.

Check your understanding

1. You can borrow my car, but only if you drive carefully.
You can borrow my car as long as you drive carefully. (or: provided that)
2. We will go hiking tomorrow if the weather is good.
We will go hiking tomorrow provided that the weather is good. (or: as long as)

In case (vs. If)

We use in case to say why somebody does (or doesn't do) something. You do something now in case something happens later.

In case ≠ If.

In case

We'll buy some more food in case Tom comes.

(= Maybe Tom will come. We'll buy some more food now, whether he comes or not; then we'll already have the food if he comes.)

If

We'll buy some more food if Tom comes.

(= Maybe Tom will come. If he comes, we'll buy some more food; if he doesn't come, we won't buy any more food.)

We use just in case for a smaller possibility:
e.g. I don't think it will rain, but I'll take an umbrella just in case. (= just in case it rains)

You can use in case + past to say why somebody did something:
e.g. I left my phone switched on in case Jane called. (= because it was possible that Jane would call)

Check your understanding

1. Take an umbrella because it might rain later. (Do it now for a future possibility)
Take an umbrella in case it rains later.
2. I left my phone switched on because it was possible that Jane would call.
I left my phone switched on in case Jane called.

In case of

In case of ... = if there is ... (especially on notices).

Examples

  • In case of fire, please leave the building as quickly as possible. (= if there is a fire)
  • In case of emergency, call this number. (= if there is an emergency)

Check your understanding

1. If there is a power cut, use the emergency lights.
In case of a power cut, use the emergency lights.
2. If there is an accident, call the police immediately.
In case of an accident, call the police immediately.

Even if (whether or not)

Even if talks about a result that will happen whether or not the condition is true.

Examples

  • Even if you apologize, she still may not forgive you.
  • You're still going to be cold even if you put on two or three jumpers.
  • We have to eat outside even if the weather is bad.

Check your understanding

1. The match will continue regardless of whether it snows.
The match will continue even if it snows.
2. She won't forgive him, regardless of how much he apologizes.
She won't forgive him even if he apologizes.

⚠️ Common Mistake

When you are talking about the future, do not use "will" after unless / as long as / so long as / provided / providing / in case.

Use a present tense.

Correct vs. Incorrect

  • I'm not going out unless it stops raining.
    (not: unless it will stop)
  • Providing the weather is good, we're going to have a picnic tomorrow.
    (not: providing the weather will be good)
  • I'll leave my phone switched on in case Jane calls.
    (not: in case Jane will call)

Check your understanding

Correct the mistake in the sentence.

1. I'll lend you the money as long as you will pay me back next week.
I'll lend you the money as long as you pay me back next week.
2. Take a map in case you will get lost.
Take a map in case you get lost.
